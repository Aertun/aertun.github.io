The proving ground for AI security work

Aertun checks what AI defenders produce — detection rules, an AI analyst's verdicts, another vendor's closures, an investigation's citations, "not affected" statements — against evidence the AI did not write, and signs the result in open formats anybody can verify: proven, refuted, or inconclusive. It replays attacks on AI agents through your detections, and runs on a complete security-operations core: collection, detection, an AI analyst that only suggests, and a tamper-evident record.

A tour, in 10 min 23 s

Every feature in the console, on the command line and through the agent interface, recorded from a real run of release 0.2.0. Captions are on by default; the transcript is below.

Transcript

This is Aertun, the proving ground for AI security work. AI now writes detection rules, triages alerts and investigates incidents. Aertun checks that work against evidence the AI did not write, and signs the result, whichever way it comes out. Everything in this tour was recorded from a real run, in Docker, of the build being released.

A store starts with one person, its Owner. aertun init prints a one-time link, and opening it adds a passkey. There is no default password and no default account. Signing in uses the passkey, and the first screen, Today, shows what is waiting for you to decide.

A proof is only as good as its evidence, so Aertun keeps its own. Events go into an append-only store, and sixty-three detection rules run over them. Here, a hundred and sixty-one events raise a hundred and two findings, ranked by risk. A finding's page marks the text that came from the log, because whoever is being detected may have written it.

Now the first proof. A model drafted a rule to catch second factors being reset in Okta, and its own examples agree with it. Held to a labelled corpus it did not write, it fires on a new starter enrolling a factor, and on someone replacing a lost phone. Refuted, with both mistakes named. The rule that ships passes the same corpus, and proof verify checks both signatures, and that the proof is about this exact file. Every command that makes a proof exits non-zero unless the verdict is proven, so each one is a gate for a pipeline.

The console's first section is the proving ground. Every kept proof is listed with its verdict, and checked again whenever it is read. At first, these proofs show a key not trusted here, because a signature says which key signed, never whose. Trusting a signing key is a privileged change. Here the only administrator asks, with a written reason. The request waits an hour, can be declined from any administrator's session, and is then approved with a passkey sign-in from the last five minutes. After that, the same proofs show a trusted signer.

Next, the AI analyst itself. proof analyst asks the configured model about forty-eight labelled cases, three times each, then plants instructions in the text a log carries, in English, German and Japanese, in base64, and more. The models here are stand-ins, so these numbers describe the method, not any real model. The cautious one was moved by none of the planted instructions. The obedient one, which does what the log tells it, was moved three times out of eleven, and one hijack is enough to refute it.

Another vendor's AI can be scored on your own analysts' decisions. Its verdicts are imported from a file, and a file with free text in it, such as the product's reasoning, is refused whole. verdict prove joins each verdict to what a person later decided, counts only verdicts given before that decision, and states an exact bound on the false-closure rate. Ten of a hundred and eighty closures were real: between three and nine percent, under the declared tolerance of ten. Proven.

Agreement with analysts is biased, because people re-examine only what looked wrong. Blind adjudication draws a random sample of every closure, commits it to the audit log before anyone sees it, and shows each item with its evidence and nothing about who closed it. A judgement is a record in the log, never a decision on the finding, so a product that learns from analysts cannot learn the answers.

A vendor's claim, such as fewer than two percent of closures wrong, can be registered as a claim card before it is tested. The metric, the threshold and the window are fixed first, and only data arriving afterwards counts. Tested while the window is still open, it decides nothing, and the proof says exactly that.

Not affected, says who? vuln prove-vex holds a vendor's VEX statements against the asset's bill of materials, its advisories and the reachability analysis. The scanner said express is not present. The bill lists express 4.18, inside the affected range. Contradicted, and refuted. A statement that the versions do support is proven.

A rule's ATT&CK tag is only its author's word. proof coverage holds each technique the rules claim to events labelled with it by whatever produced them, such as an Atomic Red Team test, and to events labelled benign. Named here, the two techniques are each caught, and nothing benign sets their rules off, so both are proven. Asked about all thirty-four techniques the rules claim, it is inconclusive: thirty-two have no labelled instance yet, and it says so instead of giving a percentage. With telemetry, it also checks that each rule would still fire on the fields its sources send today. A proven proof can be issued as an in-toto verification result, so a policy engine gates on one property.

Supply-chain attacks now come through packages people already trust. vuln continuity holds each npm, PyPI and Go package in a bill to the release before it: its provenance, its trusted publishing, its source and its install scripts. This bill names nine packages, read live from their registries. Seven held. The compromised axios release of March, and the package it pulled in, are refuted, because npm has withdrawn both.

Maintainers are buried in AI-written vulnerability reports. vuln referee holds a report to a checkout of the source it names. A real report on minimist 1.2.5 is proven: its function exists, its version is in range, and its CVSS vector gives 9.8, critical, as it says. Its digest was committed to the audit log first, which fixes its priority. A made-up report on the same package is refuted on three counts: its range excludes the version, the file and function it cites are not there, and its own vector does not give the score it claims.

Alerts from Wazuh, or any monitor, can be sealed as they arrive. Here sixteen alerts a real Wazuh manager raised are stored, and sealed in one audit entry. proof sealed finds one of them, exactly as Wazuh wrote it, in that batch. Until a head of the log is anchored in a Bitcoin block, it is inconclusive, and says the time rests on the log alone.

A detection provider, Wazuh included, can be tested blind. mdr plan draws emulations at random times, and commits only the plan's digest to the audit log before any of them runs, so nobody can tune for the test.

When an AI writes up an investigation, proof evidence runs every citation again against the event store. A report whose claims all hold is proven. One that miscounts is refuted, and names the claim. Nothing the report's author wrote reaches the proof, except identifiers and numbers.

Agents are registered before they act. This one is given a token bound to a single run, and uses the agent interface as an agent would. It finds the operations, reads the queue and the proofs, and its proposal is refused, because it may only read. agent reconcile then holds the agent's own telemetry against what the server saw. It reported nothing, and was seen calling four times, so it is refuted, call by call. When an agent asks to act for a person, that person sees what it would reach, for how long, and allows or refuses. Every agent has a kill switch. A responder stops it with a reason, and its tokens are revoked in the same act.

The agent interface is twelve operations behind three tools, which an agent loads only as it searches for them. Eleven read, and one proposes a decision for a person to make. An agent can check a proof with verify_proof. Change one character, and the proof is refused. Agents cannot approve anything, sign or keep a proof, or reach another platform, and every value they read says whether someone outside could have written it.

The wind tunnel replays scripted attacks on AI agents: an injection through an issue, an MCP server's rug pull, data walking out, a slopsquatted package. It scores whether your own Sigma rules catch them, with respelled variants of each session. Against Aertun's own rule pack it is self-graded, and it says so.

Underneath is a complete security-operations core. Events, and how far behind each source runs. Hunting by rarity, over the store. Detection rules with fixtures, a trial ring, and each rule's measured record. Incidents, with the notification clocks of GDPR, NIS2, DORA and the Cyber Resilience Act. And vulnerabilities, ranked by exploitation, with the smallest upgrade that clears the most risk.

People are invited with a role: viewer, analyst, responder or admin, and three jobs beside them. There are deliberately no custom roles. auth explain says exactly what a person may do, and why. Everybody signs in with two factors: a passkey, or single sign-on through OpenID Connect or SAML, with presets for the common identity providers.

Every change is written to an append-only audit log: a hash chain and a Merkle tree, with heads signed in Ed25519 and ML-DSA. auditlog verify re-hashes every entry. auditlog prove gives seven hashes that prove one entry is in the log. And an evidence pack for an SP 800-53 control is verified offline, by someone who never sees the log.

Finally, the claim that anyone can check a proof without Aertun. This script builds the signed bytes by hand, checks the Ed25519 signature with Python's cryptography library, and the post-quantum ML-DSA-65 signature with OpenSSL. Both hold.

The console follows Material 3, in light and dark, and works at phone width. All sixty-one of its pages in this run were checked in Chrome for labels, headings, landmarks, keyboard order and WCAG contrast, and no issue was found.

Aertun is open source, under the Apache licence: one binary, with no dependencies. The manual is at aertun.github.io.

Built so that nobody, and no model, acts alone

AI that cannot be talked into anything

The AI analyst's plan is fixed in code before it reads a log line, its answer is one word from a closed set, and the reason a person reads is built from counts, so text an attacker wrote never reaches the screen of whoever decides.

Two people for what matters

An action on another platform is approved by somebody other than whoever asked, and making an administrator, issuing an administrator's token or weakening a setting takes a second administrator — or, for an only administrator, a delay anybody can stop. Agents propose; people decide.

A record nobody can quietly change

Every change is in an append-only audit log: a hash chain, tree heads signed with Ed25519 and ML-DSA, and the head committed to Bitcoin.
