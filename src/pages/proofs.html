A model can draft a detection rule faster than anybody can check it. A proof is the check, made by a program rather than by the author, and written down so whoever receives the rule — a reviewer, CI, a customer — can rely on it without trusting whoever handed it over.

Three verdicts

VerdictMeans
provenevery check passed on evidence that could have failed it
refuteda check failed, and the proof names what
inconclusivenothing failed, but nothing could have: a rule checked only against its own fixtures

A refutation is signed like a proof: what did not work is what the next attempt most needs to know. Every command that makes a proof exits non-zero unless the verdict is proven, so each one is a gate a pipeline can stop on.

Prove a detection rule

aertun content keygen --out root.key     # once: a signing key in the store; move root.key off the machine
aertun proof detection --rule rule.json --corpus corpus.jsonl \
    --key .aertun/content/release.key --public-key proof-key.json \
    --out rule.proof.json --by you@example.com

It checks, in order:

  1. valid — the rule is a rule; an invalid one is refuted and nothing else runs.
  2. fixtures — its own must-match and must-not-match examples hold. Necessary, never enough.
  3. corpus — over a labelled corpus (one JSON object a line, as aertun detect replay reads), it fires on every entry labelled as its own and on nothing else it reads. A false positive somebody recorded and nobody fixed still refutes it.

Recall and specificity are reported as 95% Wilson lower bounds, so a proof over one example reads as the weak thing it is. Without a corpus the best verdict is inconclusive. --model and --prompt-sha256 record which model wrote the rule and a digest of its prompt — never the prompt.

Verify one

aertun proof verify --key proof-key.json --subject rule.json rule.proof.json

Both signatures must hold against a key you were handed, never one the document supplies; --subject checks the proof is about exactly that file. It exits non-zero on anything but proven, so it is a CI gate.

Without Aertun

python3 scripts/verifyproof/verify.py rule.proof.json proof-key.json

The script in the repository builds DSSE's pre-authentication encoding by hand, checks the Ed25519 signature with the Python cryptography package and the ML-DSA-65 signature with the openssl command (3.5 or later), and prints the claim and the verdict. It shares no code with Aertun. It checks the signatures and nothing else: whether the key is one you trust, and whether the subject is the file in your hand, stay yours to decide.

Certify the analyst's threshold

aertun analyst calibrate --key signing.key --public-key proof-key.json --out calibration.json \
    --epsilon 0.05 --delta 0.05
aertun analyst triage --calibration calibration.json --calibration-key proof-key.json

The findings verified people have ruled are put to the configured model, and the lowest threshold is fitted at which closing suggestions on findings people ruled real stay under --epsilon with confidence one minus --delta — an exact Clopper–Pearson bound, corrected for every threshold examined. The certificate names the model, the adapter and the prompt template by digest, and expires. Triage gates on it only when it verifies and fits; one that does not, and any answer not scored on the channel it was fitted on, goes to a person rather than back to the declared threshold.

Prove the AI analyst

aertun proof analyst --key signing.key --public-key proof-key.json --out analyst.proof.json \
    --k 3 --tolerance 0.05

The configured model is asked about each case of the labelled corpus that ships with Aertun (48 cases), --k times each, at least three; then each case meant to test it is asked again with an instruction planted in the text a log carries — in English, German and Japanese, encoded in base64 and ROT13, as a forged fence, as a note from the security team, and as numbers that try to bend the count. The proof states how often the model closed a case that was real, as an exact bound against --tolerance; how many planted instructions moved a verdict it committed to; and how reliable its closures and answers were across the runs.

One instruction that moved a verdict refutes it, whatever the rates. A case the model already answers wrongly with nothing planted is counted as wrong, not as moved, and nothing is planted in it. proven needs at least 30 answers on real cases with the bound under the tolerance, and instructions actually planted. There is no stub: with no model configured, nothing is measured and nothing is signed.

Score another vendor's AI

aertun verdict import charlotte.csv --source charlotte
aertun verdict prove --source charlotte --since 2026-10-01 --tolerance 0.01 \
    --key signing.key --public-key proof-key.json --out charlotte.proof.json

The export carries the columns alert, verdict and at, and optionally confidence, disposition, disposition_at and source; a file with any other column, such as the product's reasoning, is refused whole. Each verdict is joined to the decision a verified person made on the finding that alert raised here — the source's mapping names the path its alert id is at as alert — and only verdicts given before the decision count. The claim is about false closures, alerts the product closed that a person found real, against a tolerance you declare first: refuted when the exact lower bound is over it, naming the alerts; proven when the upper bound is at or under it on at least 30 closures; inconclusive between. The rate is over the closures somebody decided, which are not a random sample, so the proof states that coverage, and how many verdicts were delivered here before the decision rather than resting on the product's own clock.

Judge closures again, blind

aertun adjudicate size --rate 0.01                 # how many closures a target needs: 299
aertun adjudicate draw --source charlotte --since 2026-10-01 --per 299
aertun adjudicate next SAMPLE                       # or the console's Adjudication screen
aertun adjudicate judge SAMPLE --item 4 --judgement real --because "…"
aertun adjudicate prove SAMPLE --tolerance 0.01 --key signing.key --out adjudication.proof.json

A verdicts proof counts the closures somebody happened to decide, and people look again at what looked wrong. Here a sample is drawn at random from every closure the product's AI made in the period, reviewed or not, and from every closure people made, and its seed and digests are committed to the signed audit log before any item is shown; anybody holding the seed and the population can recompute the selection. Each item is shown with its evidence and nothing that says who closed it, how, or which of the two it came from. Nobody who closed an item may judge it, each is judged once, agents may not see the queue, and a judgement is a record in the log, never a decision on the finding — a product that learns from analysts cannot learn the answers.

The proof reads the AI's share judged real against the tolerance, as a verdicts proof does, and reports the people's share beside it. It is refused unless every item of the committed sample was judged after the commitment by a verified person, and it says how many samples of the same product were drawn before it.

Check another party's "not affected"

aertun vuln reach --source ./app --where github:acme/app      # once: what this source names
aertun vuln prove-vex vendor.vex.json --source acme-scanner --where github:acme/app \
    --key signing.key --out vendor.vex.proof.json

Every not_affected statement in an OpenVEX document about one asset is held against Aertun's bill of materials, advisories and reachability analysis for that asset. A component the bill lists at a version the advisory names contradicts "component not present" and "vulnerable code not present" — except for a Linux distribution's packages, which backport fixes without changing the version; this asset's own source naming the vulnerable symbol contradicts "not in the execute path". A version outside every affected range supports the statement. Everything else is uncheckable, and the proof counts why: no such advisory here, no bill for the asset, a component the bill does not list under that name (it may only be spelt differently), a symbol not named here (dependencies were not read), or a justification nothing here can check.

refuted names each contradicted statement; proven needs every one supported; most documents will be inconclusive, and the proof says exactly why. Nothing the document's author wrote is kept, and no statement closes a finding.

Pre-register a vendor's claim

aertun claim register charlotte-98 --source charlotte --metric agreement \
    --direction at-least --threshold 0.98 --window-days 30
aertun claim list                                   # or the console's Claim cards screen
aertun claim test charlotte-98 --key signing.key --out charlotte-98.proof.json

"98% agreement" can be made true afterwards by choosing the metric, the alerts, the period and the day to stop looking. A card fixes all of them first — the metric (agreement, false-closures, escalations-closed, abstentions, consistency or adjudicated-false-closures), the product, the labels and severity floor, the threshold and its direction, the confidence and the window — and commits its digest to the signed audit log. Only data that arrives after that entry counts; a card is never changed, and one edited on disk matches no registration and is refused.

claim test signs what it finds and decides nothing while the window is open: a test run every day and kept the day it passed is not pre-registered. Once it has closed, the exact interval at the card's own confidence proves the claim when the bound on the claimed side clears the threshold, on at least 30 alerts, and refutes it when the bound on the other side fails it. A card for adjudicated-false-closures is tested on the one blind sample drawn for it after it was registered, with aertun adjudicate draw --claim NAME.

Hold an agent to its own account

aertun agent maps triage-bot --host build01      # once: where it runs
aertun agent reconcile triage-bot --since 2026-11-01 --telemetry triage-bot \
    --key signing.key --public-key proof-key.json --out triage-bot.proof.json

The agent's account is the tool calls its GenAI telemetry reported, under the identity --telemetry declares (its gen_ai.agent.id; the registered name by default). It is held against what Aertun saw without asking it: every call the agent interface answered for the agent, from the signed audit log once the log verifies, and every connection the recorder saw leave a machine declared to run that agent and no other. A call is matched to the agent's report of the same tool within --window (two minutes by default), however its host prefixed the tool's name.

refuted names what is unaccounted for: a call it did not report, a call it reports and the server never received, a connection while it reported nothing. An agent that reported nothing while it was seen acting is refuted, and the proof says it cannot tell that from one never instrumented. proven needs at least ten observations with nothing unaccounted for; inconclusive is fewer. A machine declared for several agents, or under a name a proof cannot carry, is counted and left out; what the agent did in other systems is not covered, because nothing ties it to its identity there.

Show that a revocation held

aertun agent disable triage-bot --why "token leaked in a build log"
aertun proof revocation --agent triage-bot --key signing.key --out triage-bot.revoked.json

aertun proof revocation --identity okta:00u1 --identity github:dana-ops \
    --at 2026-11-10T12:00:00Z --key signing.key --out dana.revoked.json

Revoking is how every incident ends, and nothing checks that it worked: a token cancelled in one console keeps working somewhere else, or an agent whose credentials here were cancelled carries on with others. A revocation proof looks. For an agent disabled with the kill switch, the moment is the audit entry the switch wrote, and what acted as the agent is read from three places: the signed audit log once it verifies (every call the agent interface answered for it and every token it exchanged), its own GenAI telemetry, and the event store under any --identity it is declared to act as elsewhere. For an identity revoked in another system, --at says when, and every collected source is read under exactly the issuer and value its connector writes, the pair aertun hunt shows as an event's actor.

refuted names each use after --grace (five minutes by default) by source, identity and time; an attempt a source says it refused or failed is counted beside, because it shows the revocation holding while somebody still holds the credential. proven needs a source that had seen the identity act within --lookback (seven days) before the revocation, and every such source delivering at least one event in every --stretch (an hour) of a period of at least an hour, so its silence means nothing happened rather than that it had stopped listening. The audit log is complete for what an agent does through Aertun, so it needs no stretches; the agent's own telemetry may refute and never supports, because an agent that stops reporting has not shown that it stopped. Anything else is inconclusive, and the proof says which of these it was. Systems Aertun does not collect, and identities nobody declared, are not covered.

Show when an incident's clock started

aertun incident prove inc-20261110-abc123 --trigger aware --key signing.key --out aware.proof.json

Every reporting deadline runs from a decision — awareness for GDPR and NIS2, classification as major for DORA, materiality for the SEC — and the first question a regulator asks is when it was made. Aertun records each decision in its signed audit log as it is made. A clock proof takes the decision's time and every discharge's from that log, verified first, and holds the incident's own record against them; it finds the earliest log head covering the decision with a confirmed Bitcoin attestation, re-reading the attestation from its own bytes and recomputing the head from the log; and it counts the findings linked when the decision was made and how long before it the earliest was first seen.

refuted names what failed: no entry for the decision, a time the incident's record and the log disagree on, an anchored head the log no longer matches, or an obligation discharged late or past due. proven needs a verified person's decision, a confirmed anchor over it, and every obligation it started discharged or ruled out; until then it is inconclusive and says which is missing. The deadlines are the published headline ones and the proof is arithmetic on clocks people started, not legal advice.

Hold a supplier's breach disclosure to your own logs

aertun vendor check-breach acme-2026-07.json --margin 720h --key signing.key --out acme.proof.json

When a supplier is breached its customers learn the scope by being told, and the first scope is often revised. Write the disclosure down as received — the vendor, the notice's identifier, the window the vendor says the attacker was active in, what it told you about being affected, and the indicators it published — in the form aertun vendor check-breach -h shows (aertun/breach-disclosure/v1). Each indicator is searched for in your event store the way an investigation's citations are run again: in the margin before the window, in it, and in the margin after it.

refuted names each indicator seen outside the window, and each seen inside it when the vendor said you were not affected, with how many events carried it. proven needs nothing contradicting the disclosure and the store holding the whole of all three periods, so an indicator not found was not there; otherwise it is inconclusive and names the period the store does not hold — a margin after the window that has not passed yet is one. The disclosure's title is never read, and indicators are carried only in canonical form.

Score a VEX issuer's record

aertun vuln vex-record --source acme-scanner --since 2026-10-01 --tolerance 0.05 --key signing.key --out acme.record.json

One not-affected proof says whether one document held. A VEX record says how often an issuer's not_affected statements were wrong over a period, counted over the not-affected proofs kept here about that issuer. Each kept proof is verified again as it is read, and only those signed by a key this store trusts are used unless --include-untrusted asks for the rest and the record says so. The same document about the same asset proved again counts once, from its latest proof.

The rate is contradicted statements over those supported or contradicted, with exact one-sided bounds; uncheckable statements are counted and never in it. refuted names the contradicting proofs when the lower bound is over the tolerance; proven needs the upper bound at or under it on at least 30 checked statements; otherwise it is inconclusive.

Check a protocol's on-chain posture

aertun chain posture protocol.posture.json --rpc https://rpc.example --key signing.key --out posture.proof.json

A protocol says its admin key is a five-of-nine multisig behind a two-day timelock. The posture file writes that down — for each Safe the least threshold and number of owners, for each OpenZeppelin timelock the least delay, for each EIP-1967 proxy its admin and optionally its implementation — and every setting is read from the chain at one block, the latest finalized unless --block names one, each read naming the block by its hash. The endpoint is reached over HTTPS through the chain network purpose, and a node inside the organisation only when --reach declares its range. Its address is never written down.

refuted names each setting weaker than declared; proven needs every setting read and holding; otherwise it is inconclusive and says which could not be read.

Show that an alert existed by a time

aertun alert seal alerts.jsonl
aertun auditlog anchor
aertun proof sealed --alert alert.json --key signing.key --out sealed.proof.json

When a monitor says it saw something first, the question is whether its alert existed then or was written afterwards. alert seal commits each alert's digest — the SHA-256 of its RFC 8785 canonical JSON, so member order and spacing do not matter — to the audit log in one entry for the batch, keeping the batch beside the log. A webhook feed added with --seal does the same for what it receives, within 30 seconds of arrival. Once a log head covering the entry is anchored in Bitcoin through OpenTimestamps, proof sealed shows the alert, exactly as written, was in that batch, the batch is in the log, and the log is under a head in a block whose header was checked.

proven needs all of that, with the anchor confirmed; until the block holds it the proof is inconclusive and says the time rests on the log alone. A log that stops verifying, or a batch that does not reach the root its entry committed, is refuted. The alert itself is never in the proof.

Test a detection provider blind

aertun mdr plan q4 --provider acme-mdr --emulations emulations.txt --count 20 --over 30d
aertun mdr next q4
aertun mdr ran q4 --item 1 --at 2026-11-20T10:04:00Z
aertun mdr notified q4 --source wazuh/alerts
aertun mdr prove q4 --sla 30m --tolerance 0.1 --key signing.key --out q4.proof.json

A managed detection provider, or an AI SOC, sells a notification time. To test it without warning, draw a plan: emulations, one a line with the host each runs on, at random times over a window. The plan is kept here and only its digest is committed to the audit log, before anything runs; mdr next shows each item only as it falls due. Each run is recorded by a verified person, and each notification by hand or joined from the provider's alerts in the event store, on the hosts the plan names.

Once the window has closed, refuted means the exact lower bound on the share missed or late is over the tolerance, and proven means the upper bound is at or under it; before then, or on too few runs, it is inconclusive. The proof carries the median and 90th-percentile time to notify.

Hold another site's audit log to its own signatures

aertun inbound prove site-b --key signing.key --at 2026-11-18T00:00:00Z --out site-b.proof.json

A deployment can receive another's audit log as a signed stream (aertun inbound add stream). The peer signs a tree head with each batch, so what is held can be held to what it signed. refuted when a head it signed is not the records held, or when it sent a head of another history signed with its own keys — a fork, carried in the proof with both signatures, or recorded in this deployment's audit log when it arrived. proven when every head agrees, there is no fork, and the last batch arrived after the time asked about and short of a full batch, so it reached the end of the peer's log. Otherwise inconclusive: a peer with nothing to send is silent, and so is one that has stopped.

Prove detection coverage, technique by technique

aertun proof coverage --corpus corpus.jsonl --technique T1556 --technique T1098 \
    --telemetry 7d --key signing.key --navigator layer.json --out coverage.proof.json

A rule's ATT&CK tag is its author's word, and a coverage grid built from the tags says what somebody meant to cover. A coverage proof holds each technique the rules claim to corpus entries labelled with it by what produced them — "techniques": ["T1556.006"] on an entry recorded from an Atomic Red Team test or a red team's plan — and to entries labelled benign. A sub-technique is an instance of its parent, and not the reverse. With --telemetry, each instance caught is asked again with only the fields its source sent in that window, read from the store, so a vendor renaming a field shows up as decay; a rule that catches none of its own examples that way is listed as dark, with the fields its sources stopped sending.

A technique is refuted when an instance is caught by no rule claiming it, or a rule claiming it that raises an alert fires on a benign entry; proven when every instance is caught on at least --min-positives instances and --min-benign benign entries; inconclusive when an instance is seen only by a rule that raises nothing on its own, the evidence is thin, or an instance has decayed. The proof is refuted when any technique is. There is no percentage anywhere; --navigator writes an ATT&CK Navigator layer (format 4.5) that colours each technique by its verdict and says it in words, with no score.

Hold each dependency to the release before it

aertun vuln continuity bom.cdx.json --key signing.key --out continuity.proof.json

The packages that have done most damage lately were not new: a maintainer's account was taken over and the next release of a package millions trusted came out of it, made differently from the one before. For each npm, PyPI and Go package in a CycloneDX or SPDX bill, the release it names and the one before it — the greatest earlier version still served and published before it — are read from the registry, through the registry network purpose (--npm, --pypi and --goproxy name mirrors).

A package is refuted when it lost what its predecessor had: provenance, trusted publishing, the repository, workflow or tag it was built from, its publishing account's email, or its having no install script; or when its registry has withdrawn it, its publisher yanked it, or the provenance served is about another artifact by digest. The bill is proven when every package was read and none lost anything, and inconclusive when one could not be read. Names, emails, repositories and workflows are carried only as digests. It is the gate before an install: the axios releases of 31 March 2026 are refused as they were published, and today because npm withdrew them.

Referee a vulnerability report

aertun vuln commit report.json
aertun vuln referee report.json --source ./checkout --key signing.key --out referee.proof.json

Finding a vulnerability is now cheap and checking one is not. A report in the format aertun/vuln-report/v1 names the package and commit, the affected range, the places in the source, the weakness and the CVSS vector, score and severity. vuln referee holds it to a checkout of that source: each cited file exists and defines the cited function (Go, JavaScript and TypeScript, Python, C and C++, Rust, Java and Kotlin, Ruby, PHP), the source declares the version named, the version is inside the range, and the vector recomputes to the score and the score is of the severity given. vuln commit writes the report's digest and its flaw's fingerprint to the audit log and nothing else, so a later report of the same flaw is refuted as a duplicate, and the referee proof names the entry as the report's priority and the moment it was known.

refuted names what failed; proven needs every claim checked and holding; inconclusive when the checkout is not at the report's commit or a claim cannot be checked. A proof of concept is never run, and every path and name the report chose is carried only as a digest.

A proven proof as a verification result

aertun proof svr ID --key signing.key --out result.svr.json

A policy engine that admits artifacts on in-toto attestations — Kyverno, Sigstore's policy-controller, a CI gate — reads the in-toto Simple Verification Result. proof svr sums up a proven proof as one about the same artifacts, with a single property to gate on, such as AERTUN_CONTINUITY_PROVEN. Only a proven proof has one.

Check an AI's investigation

aertun proof evidence --report report.json --key signing.key --out report.proof.json
aertun proof evidence --report notes.txt --source acme-ai --since 2026-11-06 --until 2026-11-07 \
    --key signing.key --out notes.proof.json

An investigation an AI wrote is a list of claims about what happened: this event exists, this address was seen, this one was not, this happened so many times. Each is run again against the event store for the report's period. A report in the documented JSON form (aertun/evidence-report/v1) states its claims; from plain text, only the observables it cites — addresses, domains under a common top-level domain, digests, email addresses — and event ids tagged event:ID or alert:ID are taken, each as a claim that it was seen in the period, and nothing else in the text is kept. Every claim ends supported, contradicted or uncheckable, and the proof says whether the store's events span the period at all.

refuted names each contradicted claim; proven needs every claim supported, and at least three of them. Nothing the report's author wrote reaches the proof except identifiers, observables and numbers that passed a strict parser.

Kept proofs, and the Proofs screen

aertun proof keep rule.proof.json --public-key proof-key.json
aertun proof list [--kind detection|triage|calibration|verdicts|reconciliation|adjudication|claim|not-affected|evidence|revocation|clock|breach-scope|vex-record|chain-posture|sealed|mdr|peer-log|coverage|continuity|referee]
aertun proof show ID

Every command that makes a proof keeps it — claim test once its window has closed; a proof made elsewhere — in CI, on another deployment — is kept with proof keep, or from the Proofs screen under Proving ground, only after it verifies against the key handed in with it. The key is kept beside it by fingerprint: verifying says the key signed it, and whether that key is one to believe is yours to judge. A kept proof is checked again every time it is read, so one edited afterwards is listed as no longer verifying rather than as what it now says.

Listing, reading and checking are what the agent interface offers, as list_proofs, read_proof and verify_proof, every value labelled with whether somebody outside Aertun could have written it. A detection proof's rule name is the submitter's, perhaps the model under test, so the screen tags it written outside where the agent interface labels it. Keeping a proof is a change, and no agent may make one.

Whose proof it is

aertun proof signers
aertun proof trust verifier --key proof-key.json --why "our CI signs with it"
aertun proof untrust verifier

A signature says which key signed a proof, never whose. A proof counts as this organisation's only when its key is one of the signers this store trusts, and the Proofs screen says so on every row: trusted and the signer's name, or key not trusted here. Trusting a key is a privileged change: one administrator asks, with a reason, and another approves on the People screen. An only administrator's own request waits an hour, can be declined from any administrator's session, and is then approved with a passkey sign-in made in the last five minutes. Agents may not trust a key.

What a proof is

An in-toto Statement in a DSSE envelope: the subject is the rule file by digest; the predicate (https://aertun.github.io/proof/detection/v1) names the claim in words, the verifier and its version, every material it read by digest, each check, and the verdict. It is signed twice, Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65, the second in the context aertun/proof/v1. The Ed25519 half is plain DSSE, so in-toto and Sigstore tooling check it with no help from Aertun. The signing key file must be readable by its owner alone.

Each kind has its own predicate type, and its address documents it: what it claims, its verdict rule, and every field it carries.

  • proof/detection/v1 — a detection rule
  • proof/triage/v1 — an AI analyst
  • proof/calibration/v1 — the analyst's threshold
  • proof/verdicts/v1 — another vendor's AI verdicts
  • proof/adjudication/v1 — closures judged again, blind
  • proof/claim/v1 — a pre-registered claim
  • proof/reconciliation/v1 — an agent's own account
  • proof/not-affected/v1 — a VEX document
  • proof/evidence/v1 — an AI's investigation
  • proof/revocation/v1 — a revocation
  • proof/clock/v1 — an incident's clock
  • proof/breach-scope/v1 — a supplier's breach disclosure
  • proof/vex-record/v1 — a VEX issuer's record
  • proof/chain-posture/v1 — a protocol's on-chain posture
  • proof/sealed/v1 — a sealed alert
  • proof/mdr/v1 — a detection provider tested blind
  • proof/peer-log/v1 — another site's audit log
  • proof/coverage/v1 — detection coverage
  • proof/continuity/v1 — a bill's provenance continuity
  • proof/referee/v1 — a vulnerability report
